Every youth program claims to offer "mentorship," but the quality of that mentorship varies enormously. Here's what actually separates an effective mentor from a well-meaning adult who shows up occasionally.

## Consistency over intensity

Young people don't need a mentor who shows up once with a big speech and disappears. They need someone who shows up reliably, over time, even when nothing dramatic is happening. Trust is built in the ordinary, repeated moments — not the one big event.

## Listening more than advising

The instinct for many adults working with youth is to jump straight to advice. The mentors who actually make a difference spend far more time listening — asking questions, letting a young person work through their own thinking — before offering a perspective.

## Sharing real struggle, not just success

Young people can tell the difference between a mentor who presents a polished, finished version of success and one who's honest about the struggle it took to get there. Mentors who share real setbacks — and how they worked through them — are far more relatable and far more useful as a model.

## Holding high expectations with real support

Effective mentorship isn't about lowering the bar to make things easier. It's about holding a young person to a high standard while making sure they have the support to actually reach it. Expectation without support feels like pressure; support without expectation feels like pity. The combination is what builds real growth.

## Respecting culture and identity

For Indigenous youth in particular, mentorship that respects and incorporates cultural identity — rather than treating it as separate from the "real" mentoring work — tends to land far more deeply than a generic program applied the same way to every community.

## Why this matters for program design

Organizations building youth mentorship programs should design for consistency and relationship over one-off events. A single assembly can plant a seed, but ongoing mentorship is what helps it grow.
