Land-based learning — camps, hunting, fishing, and traditional activities on the land — has been part of Indigenous knowledge systems for generations. It's also one of the most effective, underused tools in youth wellness programming today.

## Confidence built through real skills

Land-based activities teach real, tangible skills — tracking, harvesting, preparing food, reading weather and terrain. Unlike many classroom accomplishments, these skills come with immediate, visible proof of competence: a young person can see exactly what they did and how it worked. That kind of confidence transfers into other areas of life.

## Connection to culture and identity

For Indigenous youth, land-based learning is also a direct connection to culture, language, and identity — often a more powerful one than anything taught in a classroom. Reconnecting with traditional practices gives young people a sense of belonging and continuity with their community and ancestors.

## A different kind of mentorship

Time on the land naturally creates space for mentorship that doesn't happen the same way in a classroom. Working alongside an elder, a camp leader, or an older peer on a shared task builds trust and openness that a scheduled "mentorship session" rarely does.

## Mental health benefits that are well documented

Time outdoors, physical activity, and a sense of purpose are all independently linked to better mental health outcomes — land-based camps combine all three. For communities dealing with high rates of youth mental health challenges, this isn't a nice extra; it's a meaningful part of the solution.

## Building land-based learning into youth programming

Land-based camps work best as part of an ongoing relationship with a community, not a one-off event. Programs that return to the same communities year after year, building trust over time, see the deepest impact.
