Taking a group of youth outside for a day is easy. Building a land-based wellness program that produces real, lasting change is a different undertaking entirely.

## Start with relationship to land, not just activity

A land-based program that's really just an outdoor activity program with a different name misses the point. The goal is to build an actual relationship between youth and the land — understanding it, respecting it, learning from it — not just using it as a backdrop for games or team-building exercises.

## Elders and knowledge keepers aren't optional extras

Programs that treat Elder involvement as a nice-to-have, brought in occasionally for a blessing or an opening prayer, miss most of the value land-based learning can offer. Elders and knowledge keepers should be central to how the program is designed, not an add-on to a program designed around something else.

## Consistency matters more than scale

A single large camp once a year generates good photos but limited lasting impact. A smaller program that brings the same group of youth back to the land repeatedly, building familiarity and relationship over time, tends to produce much deeper change — even if it reaches fewer youth overall.

## Safety and logistics are part of the program, not separate from it

Land-based programming done properly requires real investment in safety training, proper equipment, and experienced staff who know the land being used. Cutting corners here isn't just a liability risk — it undermines the program's core message, since rushed or unsafe logistics communicate that the land (and the youth) aren't being taken seriously.

## Measure connection, not just attendance

Most program evaluation defaults to counting how many youth showed up. A more useful measure is whether participants are developing an actual ongoing relationship with land-based practices — are they continuing activities like hunting, fishing, or spending time outdoors on their own, after the formal program ends?
