Imposter syndrome — the nagging feeling that you don't actually belong, despite evidence to the contrary — isn't unique to any one field. Athletes who've made it to the highest level of competitive sport often describe the same self-doubt as anyone stepping into a new role at work.

## Doubt doesn't mean you don't belong

Reaching a new level — a promotion, a scholarship, a professional opportunity — almost always comes with a stretch of feeling out of your depth. That feeling is common precisely because you're being tested against a higher standard than before, not because you don't deserve to be there.

## Competence comes before confidence

A common misconception is waiting to feel confident before acting. In reality, competence usually comes first — through repetition, practice, and showing up even when you don't feel ready — and confidence follows. Athletes rarely feel fully confident walking into their first game at a new level; they build that confidence by playing anyway.

## Comparison is the trap

Imposter syndrome gets worse when you compare your behind-the-scenes effort to someone else's highlight reel. Every person who looks like they belong effortlessly is dealing with their own doubts you simply don't see.

## Having people in your corner

One of the most effective counters to imposter syndrome is having people around you — a coach, mentor, teammate, or colleague — who can see your growth when you can't see it yourself, and remind you of it when self-doubt creeps in.

## Reframing the feeling

Imposter syndrome often signals that you're growing, not that you've made a mistake. Learning to recognize the feeling as a normal part of stretching into something new — rather than proof you don't belong — makes it far easier to keep moving forward instead of shrinking back.
